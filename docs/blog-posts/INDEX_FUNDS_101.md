# What Makes a Good Stock Index?

I built the **Fattore Indexes**, a set of free-float market-cap-weighted benchmarks for broad, low-cost index funds. You can see them here: [fattorestreet.com/indexes](https://fattorestreet.com/indexes).

## What is a stock index?

An index is a collection/portfolio of stocks picked and weighted by a set of rules. Many of the most popular investment vehicles today are **index funds**, meaning they are funds that aim to follow an index as a benchmark.

## What makes a good index?

### 1. Passively managed

The best indexes are passively managed, which means the index follows a fixed set of rules and then sticks to those rules no matter what. Emotions and bias stay out of the equation. For example, most major indexes only include shares available on a major exchange (NASDAQ, NYSE, or CBOE).

### 2. Broad

A strong index represents the vast majority of the entire stock market. Broad diversification means no matter what you are gonna get your fair share of market returns. Broad indexes have historically delivered strong returns with surprisingly low volatility compared to single stocks.

> When looking for a needle in a haystack, just buy the whole haystack.

### 3. Free-float market-cap weighted

Market capitalization weighted indexes size each position by how large the company is in the market. As of April 12, 2026, Nvidia has a market cap of ~$4.47 Trillion and so has about 10x the weight of Netflix with a market cap of ~$431 Billion. This creates an accurate representation of the market as a portfolio.

The best indexes are *free-float* market-cap-weighted, meaning the market cap is essentially based on the part of the company that is public. This ensures the stock has enough liquidity to actually construct a fund from the index.

## Low cost is king

Investing has never been easier. These days you can trade commission free and buy funds with minimal fees. The main fee of owning an index fund is the management cost applied each year. The **expense ratio** of an electronically traded fund (ETF) or mutual fund is the cost to the shareholder each year represented as a percentage of your total holdings.

An advisor that charges 1% each year on a $1 million portfolio is taking $10,000 out each year, regardless of how the market performs. The lower the fees, the better your returns will be. There are plenty of funds that charge less than a 0.05% expense ratio, at most $500 on $1 million.

| Tier | Indexes |

| **A** | S&P 500 |
| **B** | NASDAQ 100 |
| **F** | Dow Jones Industrial Average |

### 🏆 S tier

The **Russell 3000**, **Dow Jones US Total Stock Market Index**, and the **CRSP US Total Market Index** all follow the above criteria and cover over 95% of the total US stock market. This makes them fantastic indexes that many of the best index funds follow.

### A tier

The **Standard & Poor's 500 (S&P 500)** is technically an actively managed index that is chosen by a committee. Tesla was actually eligible in July 2020, but wasn't added until December 2020, missing the gains Tesla posted during those five months.

### B tier

The **NASDAQ 100** leaves some to be desired because it only includes stocks in the NASDAQ and excludes financial companies. These are arbitrary choices that leave out a big chunk of US stocks. It is also a *modified* cap-weighted index, meaning it has a few extra rules that diverge from the norm.

### F tier

The **Dow** is not a good index:

- It is actively managed by a committee that chooses the stocks.
- It isn't broad, only containing 30 stocks.
- It is weighted by **stock price**, which doesn't make it a direct representation of the market.

Sherwin-Williams has approximately 1.8x more weight in the Dow than Nvidia does, despite having a market cap roughly 45x smaller.
