# ETF vs Mutual Funds

_FattoreStreet @ [`c1b6fe73`](https://github.com/JohnFattore/FattoreStreet/tree/c1b6fe731f5513783be6b22c218ba1eab010b345) — 2026-05-12_

What's actually different between an exchange traded fund (ETF) and a mutual fund? Both are pooled fund vehicles that can track broad indexes at low costs. Vanguard, for example, has VTI and VTSAX, which both track the CRSP US Total Market Index at an ultra low cost. For disciplined index fund investors, the difference is small and both types provide exceptional options for retail investors. While they are similar products, there are some differences to be aware of to make more confident investment decisions.

## How they trade
Mutual funds are only traded once a day after the market closes and only ever between the fund and the investor. A mutual fund investor buys newly created shares from the fund at exactly net asset value (NAV). Net asset value is the underlying value of all the assets the fund owns and can be seen as the intrinsic value of the fund. ETFs are traded like stocks on exchanges and can be traded between any two parties during trading hours. This means ETFs have a bid ask spread and their value can deviate from NAV. A bid ask spread is the gap between the highest price a buyer will pay (bid) and the lowest price a seller will accept (ask).

## Why mutual funds distribute capital gains
Mutual fund shares are created when an investor buys into the fund. When an investor cashes out their mutual fund shares, they sell them back to the fund, who needs to produce the cash to pay them. The fund might have to sell assets and realize gains in order to do this. These realized gains get passed onto the investors of the fund, which is true for any capital gains either of these funds incur. Mutual fund investors are forced to recognize this gain, even if they didn't sell any of their position. The cash is delivered back to them, which has the benefit of a step up in cost basis so they are not taxed on this gain again.

## In-Kind Redemption, how ETFs avoid distributing capital gains
ETF shares are created differently, which helps them avoid realizing gains when investors cash out. In-kind redemption is the process in which big firms called authorized participants (APs) trade a basket of assets for equivalent shares of the ETF. This process also works in reverse, APs can trade shares of the ETF for an equivalent basket of assets. This allows the ETF issuer to adjust the number of outstanding ETF shares without forcing holders to realize capital gains. 

## Arbitrage
This process also ensures the ETF stays around its NAV. When the ETF is trading below NAV, APs can buy up ETF shares and redeem them for the basket of assets. When the ETF price is above NAV, APs can redeem a basket of assets for new ETF shares. APs are incentivized to do this because they pocket the difference from the arbitrage. The practice pushes the price of the ETF towards its NAV. This practice is entirely legal, widely used across the industry, and a key reason broad-market ETFs almost never distribute capital gains to their shareholders. This allows the money to continue to compound.

## Heartbeat trade
A particular process called a heartbeat trade allows ETFs to wipe out unrealized gains. The mechanic works like this: an AP delivers a large cash injection to the ETF in exchange for newly created shares, the fund then hands back its most appreciated stocks in a redemption basket a day or two later, and the AP exits the position. The result is that highly appreciated stock leaves the fund without a taxable event, and shareholders never see a capital gains distribution from the rebalance.

## Portability
Mutual funds can be a bit less portable than ETFs. ETFs can generally be transferred in-kind to any major brokerage, meaning no sale is forced and no capital gains incurred. Mutual funds don’t share this flexibility; in-kind transfers between brokerages require specific agreements that aren't always in place.

## Which should you choose
This is splitting hairs for disciplined index fund investors. Both wrappers offer funds that are low cost, track broad indexes, and deliver excellent long-term returns. When comparing the popular index funds versions of each, the differences tend to melt away. Mutual funds can cause untimely capital gains, but funds tracking broad indexes have very little turnover and net inflows large enough that they often distribute nothing year after year. For example, Fidelity's FSKAX, which tracks the Dow Jones U.S. Total Stock Market Index, didn't distribute any capital gains in 2025. This also becomes a moot point in tax-advantaged accounts such as an IRA or 401(k), where tax is always deferred even if capital gains are realized. ETFs carry a spread and can trade away from NAV, but large ETFs like VTI carry small spreads and trade in lockstep with NAV.

### The best choice is whichever one keeps you invested.
