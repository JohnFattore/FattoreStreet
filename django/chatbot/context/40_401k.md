# 401(k) and other workplace plans

Sources: IRS 401(k) Resource Guide, Publication 575 and Publication 590-B. Most of these rules
also apply to 403(b) plans; 457(b) plans differ in places, so say so when it matters. This file
holds the rules; dollar limits change every year and are not here. If no limits file for the year
in question is in this reference, say you don't have that year's figures.

## Contributing

- Employees choose a percentage of pay to defer, as pre-tax (traditional), Roth, or both if the
  plan offers Roth.
- **The employee deferral limit is per person, not per plan.** Someone with two jobs in a year
  shares one limit across all their 401(k) and 403(b) plans. A 457(b) plan has its own separate
  limit, so a government worker with both can contribute to each.
- A higher overall limit caps employee plus employer contributions combined.
- Catch-up contributions are allowed from age 50, with a larger catch-up for ages 60 through 63.
- **High earners' catch-ups must be Roth.** Employees whose prior-year FICA wages from that
  employer exceeded $145,000 (indexed yearly) must make catch-up contributions as Roth. The rule
  takes effect in 2026 (plans must comply in good faith; the final regulations generally apply
  from 2027). If the plan has no Roth option, those employees can't make catch-up contributions
  at all.
- Some plans allow **after-tax (non-Roth) contributions** above the deferral limit. If the plan
  also allows converting them to Roth in the plan or withdrawing them to a Roth IRA, this is the
  "mega backdoor Roth." Only some plans support it; the user must check their plan documents.

## The employer match

- Contribute at least enough to get the full match. It's an immediate, guaranteed return that
  nothing else matches, and skipping it is leaving pay on the table.
- **Vesting:** your own contributions are always 100% yours. Employer contributions can follow a
  vesting schedule (at most 3-year cliff or 6-year graded). Leaving before vesting forfeits the
  unvested part, which is worth knowing before changing jobs.
- Employers may now match student loan payments as if they were 401(k) deferrals, if the plan
  adopts it.
- Employers may let employees take match contributions as Roth; those are taxable income in the
  year contributed.

## Traditional vs Roth 401(k)

- Same trade-off as IRAs: traditional saves tax now and is taxed on withdrawal; Roth is taxed now
  and withdrawn tax-free. Compare today's tax rate with the expected rate in retirement.
- Roth 401(k)s have no income limit, unlike Roth IRAs, so high earners can use them directly.
- Roth 401(k)s no longer have required minimum distributions during the owner's lifetime (since
  2024).
- A Roth 401(k) has its own 5-year clock for qualified distributions, separate from any Roth IRA.

## Investing inside the plan

- Choices are limited to the plan's menu. Look for broad index funds with expense ratios under
  0.10%; when the menu is poor, a low-cost target date fund is usually the best simple choice.
- Pick one target date fund and hold it alone. Mixing it with other funds undoes the automatic
  allocation it exists to provide.

## Early access

- **Rule of 55:** if you leave your job (quit, laid off or retired) in or after the calendar year
  you turn 55, withdrawals from **that employer's** plan avoid the 10% penalty (age 50, or 25
  years of service, for qualified public safety employees). It doesn't apply to earlier
  employers' plans or to IRAs, so rolling the account into an IRA before 59½ gives it up.
- Most other early withdrawals before 59½ owe income tax plus the 10% penalty. Shared exceptions
  with IRAs include death, disability, substantially equal periodic payments (after leaving the
  employer), medical expenses above 7.5% of AGI, birth or adoption, emergency personal expense,
  domestic abuse, terminal illness and disaster distributions. Several of these (and hardship
  withdrawals and loans below) exist only if the plan adopts them, so the user should check with
  their plan. Unlike IRAs, workplace plans have no exception for higher education or a first
  home.
- **Hardship withdrawals** are allowed by some plans for immediate, heavy financial need. They're
  still taxed and usually still penalized, and can't be paid back.
- **401(k) loans**, if the plan offers them: up to the lesser of $50,000 or 50% of the vested
  balance, repaid through payroll within 5 years (longer for buying a main home). Interest goes
  back into your own account. If you leave the job with a loan outstanding, the plan usually
  subtracts the unpaid balance from your account (a "loan offset"), and that offset counts as a
  taxable distribution. You can avoid the tax by putting the same amount into an IRA or new plan
  by your tax filing deadline, including extensions, for that year. Loans
  beat hardship withdrawals, but money out of the market misses its growth.

## Leaving a job

The options for an old 401(k):

1. **Leave it** in the old plan, if the balance is over $7,000 (smaller balances can be cashed
   out or moved to an IRA by the plan).
2. **Roll it into the new employer's plan**, if accepted. Keeps everything in one place and
   avoids the backdoor Roth pro-rata problem.
3. **Roll it into an IRA**, usually for the widest, cheapest fund choice. Pre-tax money goes to a
   traditional IRA, Roth money to a Roth IRA.
4. **Cash it out.** Almost always a mistake: the plan withholds 20% for tax, the whole amount is
   taxed as income, and the 10% penalty applies before 59½ (unless the rule of 55 does).

Always use a **direct rollover** (plan to plan or plan to IRA). If the check is made out to you,
20% is withheld and you must make up that 20% from other money within 60 days to avoid tax on it.

## Required minimum distributions

- Pre-tax 401(k) balances follow the same RMD ages as IRAs (73 if born 1951 to 1959, 75 if born
  1960 or later).
- **Still-working exception:** if you still work for the employer and don't own more than 5% of
  the company, RMDs from that employer's plan can wait until you retire. This doesn't apply to
  IRAs or to old employers' plans.

## Self-employed

People with self-employment income can open a **solo 401(k)** (no employees other than a spouse)
or a **SEP IRA**. A solo 401(k) usually allows more at lower incomes, because the owner
contributes both as employee and as employer, and it can include a Roth option.
