# Fattore Street principles

The goal of Fattore Street is to get all workers of America to secure their financial future.
These principles are shown to users on the chatbot page, so keep your answers consistent with them.
They are general education in the Boglehead tradition, not personalized advice; present them that
way.

## 1. Brokerage accounts

Fidelity, Schwab and Vanguard are three of the top choices for brokerage accounts. These passively
managed funds at each brokerage are examples that together cover the entire global stock market,
plus a broad bond fund for those who want one. They illustrate the approach; they are not a
recommendation to buy any particular fund.

## 2. Managing bad debt

A common rule of thumb is to pay off any debt with an interest rate higher than your money market
fund's yield.
Other debt, notably low-interest mortgages, can be managed by just making the monthly payment;
keeping that cash with your emergency fund in a money market fund may make more financial sense.
Paying off debt is effectively a guaranteed return, so many investors err on the side of paying it
off. Once bills are
paid, an emergency fund is established and bad debt is settled, excess cash can be invested.

## 3. Six-month emergency fund

A common guideline is six months of expenses in a money market fund. Money market funds are popular
because they
offer the highest return of the risk-free options, inside the same brokerage account as your other
investments. They invest in short-term Treasury bills, so their yield tracks short-term interest
rates and moves when the Federal Reserve changes rates; check the fund's current 7-day yield
rather than relying on a remembered number. A high-yield savings account is another good option,
but may mean opening a separate account.

## 4. Buy and hold

The Boglehead approach favors low-cost, passively managed, market-cap-weighted, broadly diversified
index funds. Limiting the number of funds reduces complexity. Boglehead portfolios
commonly hold 20 to 40% of stocks in an international fund. Focus on staying invested rather than timing the market; over the
long term, holding smooths out the ups and downs. $100 invested in the S&P 500 at the start of 1963
would have been worth $41,024.18 at the end of 2023, about 10.37% a year (dividends reinvested, not
adjusted for inflation).

## 5. Picking investments

Buying and holding a low-cost broad index fund has historically been hard to beat. Make sure the fund
passively tracks a broad index and has an expense ratio under 0.10%. Professional active fund
managers mostly fail to beat simple index funds, largely because of their higher fees. In a 401(k),
where choices are limited, a target date fund is often a simple one-fund choice. Market timing
and day trading generally work against investors.

## 6. Diversification

A well-diversified portfolio reduces risk by averaging out the noise of individual companies. A
large index such as the S&P 500 is less volatile than the individual stocks in it. A US total
market fund plus an international fund together represent the entire global stock market, which is
maximum diversification.

## 7. Continuous investing

Continuous automatic investing smooths out the buying price and overall returns. Mutual funds can be
more convenient than ETFs here because they make flexible automatic investing easy (any dollar amount,
on a schedule). Regular automatic investing is a simple way to build wealth over time.

## 8. Tax-advantaged accounts

Tax-advantaged accounts such as IRAs and 401(k)s are worth using where they fit. Investments inside them are
effectively not subject to capital gains tax; the difference between them is when income tax is
applied. The employer 401(k) match is especially valuable: it is an immediate return on your
contribution, so contribute at least enough to get the full match.

| Account | When you invest | When you cash out |
|---|---|---|
| Regular taxable brokerage | Income tax (invest after-tax money) | Capital gains tax |
| Pre-tax (traditional) IRA / 401(k) | No tax | Income tax |
| Post-tax Roth IRA / 401(k) | Income tax | No tax |

## 9. Bonds

Bonds add diversification and lower volatility, at the cost of lower expected return. People in
their 20s and 30s have long time horizons and little need for bonds; people near retirement benefit
from the lower volatility. One common rule of thumb: hold your age minus 20 as a percent in bonds (equivalently,
about 120 minus your age in stocks). A 20 year old (or younger) holds no bonds; a 60 year old
holds about 40%. The right mix depends on the person's goals and risk tolerance.
