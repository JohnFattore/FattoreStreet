# IRAs: traditional and Roth

Sources: IRS Publication 590-A (contributions) and 590-B (distributions). This file holds the
rules, which rarely change. Dollar limits and income phase-out ranges change every year and are
not here; if no limits file for the year in question is in this reference, say you don't have
that year's figures and point the user to IRS.gov.

## Contributing

- You need taxable compensation (wages, self-employment income) to contribute. The yearly
  contribution can't exceed the lesser of the annual limit or your taxable compensation for the
  year. Investment income, pensions and Social Security don't count.
- A spouse with little or no income can contribute to their own IRA based on the working spouse's
  compensation (a "spousal IRA"), if they file jointly.
- The annual limit is shared across all of a person's traditional and Roth IRAs combined, not per
  account. People 50 and older get an extra catch-up amount.
- There is no age limit for contributing to a traditional or Roth IRA, as long as you have
  compensation.
- Contributions for a tax year can be made until that year's tax filing deadline (usually April
  15 of the next year). Filing extensions don't extend it.
- Over-contributing triggers a 6% excise tax on the excess for every year it stays in the
  account. Fix it by withdrawing the excess and its earnings by the filing deadline, including
  extensions (usually October 15).

## Traditional vs Roth

- **Traditional:** contributions may be tax-deductible now; withdrawals in retirement are taxed as
  ordinary income. Anyone with compensation can contribute, but the deduction phases out at higher
  incomes if you (or your spouse) are covered by a workplace retirement plan. A contribution that
  isn't deductible is "nondeductible" and must be reported on Form 8606 so it isn't taxed again
  later.
- **Roth:** contributions are made with after-tax money and are never deductible; qualified
  withdrawals, including all growth, are tax-free. The ability to contribute directly phases out
  at higher incomes.
- **Rule of thumb for choosing:** if your tax rate now is higher than you expect in retirement,
  traditional tends to win; if it's lower (common early in a career), Roth tends to win. When
  unsure, splitting between both hedges the bet. Roth also has no required minimum distributions
  for the owner and leaves heirs tax-free money.

## Roth withdrawals and the two 5-year rules

- Withdrawals come out in a fixed order: your contributions first, then conversions (oldest first),
  then earnings.
- **Contributions can be withdrawn at any time, for any reason, with no tax or penalty.** This is
  why some people treat a Roth IRA as a backup emergency fund, though money taken out can't be put
  back except as a new contribution within that year's limit.
- **Earnings** come out tax- and penalty-free only in a *qualified distribution*: at least 5 years
  since January 1 of the tax year of your first-ever Roth IRA contribution or conversion
  (whichever came first), **and** you are 59½
  or older, disabled, using up to $10,000 (lifetime) for a first home, or the account holder has
  died.
- **Each conversion has its own 5-year clock** for the 10% penalty: converted amounts withdrawn
  within 5 years of the conversion (counted from January 1 of the conversion year) owe the 10%
  penalty if you are under 59½. This clock is separate from the first rule.

## Conversions and the backdoor Roth

- You can convert any amount from a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA at any income. The pre-tax
  portion converted is taxed as ordinary income that year. A conversion can't be undone
  (recharacterization of conversions ended after 2017).
- **Backdoor Roth:** someone whose income is too high for a direct Roth contribution makes a
  nondeductible traditional IRA contribution, then converts it to Roth. It's legal and common.
- **The pro-rata rule is the trap.** For a conversion, the IRS treats all of your traditional,
  SEP and SIMPLE IRAs as one pot, valued at December 31 of the conversion year. If that pot holds
  pre-tax money, most of the conversion becomes taxable. People with pre-tax IRA balances often
  roll them into their current employer's 401(k) first (if the plan accepts roll-ins), since 401(k)
  balances don't count toward the pro-rata calculation. Form 8606 reports all of this.

## Early withdrawals (before 59½)

Pre-tax money withdrawn from an IRA before 59½ is taxed as income **plus** a 10% additional tax,
unless an exception applies. Common IRA exceptions:

- Death or total and permanent disability
- Substantially equal periodic payments (a "72(t)" schedule, which must continue for 5 years or
  until 59½, whichever is longer)
- Unreimbursed medical expenses above 7.5% of adjusted gross income
- Health insurance premiums while unemployed
- Qualified higher education expenses
- First-time home purchase, up to $10,000 lifetime
- Birth or adoption, up to $5,000 per child, within a year of the event
- One emergency personal expense distribution per year, up to $1,000 (or the balance above
  $1,000, if less)
- Victims of domestic abuse, up to the lesser of $10,000 (indexed yearly) or 50% of the account
- Terminal illness
- Federally declared disaster losses, up to $22,000 per disaster
- An IRS levy, or qualified military reservists called to active duty

The "rule of 55" does **not** apply to IRAs; it's a workplace-plan exception (see the 401(k)
section). Rolling a 401(k) into an IRA before 59½ gives it up.

## Required minimum distributions (RMDs)

- Traditional IRA owners must start withdrawing each year at age 73 if born 1951 to 1959, or age
  75 if born 1960 or later.
- The first RMD can wait until April 1 of the year after you reach RMD age; every later one is due
  by December 31. Delaying the first means taking two in one year, which can push you into a
  higher bracket.
- The amount is the prior December 31 balance divided by an IRS life-expectancy factor.
- Missing an RMD costs a 25% excise tax on the shortfall, cut to 10% if corrected promptly
  (generally within two years).
- Roth IRAs have no RMDs during the owner's lifetime.
- **Qualified charitable distributions (QCDs):** from age 70½, IRA money sent directly to a
  charity is excluded from income (up to an annual, indexed limit) and counts toward the RMD. For
  charitably inclined retirees this usually beats withdrawing and then donating.

## Inherited IRAs

- A surviving spouse can treat the IRA as their own, or roll it into their own IRA. Once it is
  their own, normal rules apply, including the 10% penalty before 59½. A spouse under 59½ who
  needs the money may do better keeping it as an inherited IRA for now.
- Most other beneficiaries (for deaths after 2019) must empty the account by the end of the 10th
  year after the owner's death. If the owner had already started RMDs, the beneficiary must also
  take yearly RMDs during those 10 years. (The IRS waived those yearly RMDs for 2021 through 2024;
  they apply from 2025.)
- "Eligible designated beneficiaries" can stretch withdrawals over their own life expectancy: a
  spouse, the owner's minor child (until 21, then the 10-year rule starts), a disabled or
  chronically ill person, or someone not more than 10 years younger than the owner.
- Withdrawals from an inherited IRA (one kept as an inherited account, not treated as the
  spouse's own) are not subject to the 10% early-withdrawal penalty.

## Moving money between accounts

- A **direct (trustee-to-trustee) transfer** is the safe way: no withholding, no deadline, no limit
  on how often.
- An **indirect (60-day) rollover**, where the money is paid to you, must be redeposited within 60
  days or it becomes a taxable (and possibly penalized) distribution. You get only one IRA-to-IRA
  indirect rollover per 12 months across all your IRAs. Prefer direct transfers.
